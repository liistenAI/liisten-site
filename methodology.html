Methodology

How risk ratings are determined

Every Liisten assessment cites this methodology by version, so the reasoning behind any rating — including one produced years ago — can always be shown. This page is that methodology: the factors, the rules, the derivation, and the practitioner's role, in specifics rather than adjectives.

Methodology v1.0 · Last reviewed 10 August 2026 · Cited in the footer of every Liisten assessment.

1. What a preliminary risk assessment is

When a new-client onboarding meeting is processed, Liisten produces a preliminary risk assessment: a draft, prepared for the practitioner's review, mapped to the risk dimensions AUSTRAC's guidance asks reporting entities to consider. It becomes the firm's assessment only when a practitioner has reviewed and signed it. Nothing is delivered to anyone until that happens.

2. The seven factors

Every assessment scores the same seven factors, in the same order. They are AUSTRAC's core money-laundering / terrorism-financing risk dimensions — customer, structure, cash, jurisdiction, channel, funds, and exposure to public office — held constant across sectors so assessments are comparable within a firm:

  • Client type — who the client is: profile, occupation or business, and how well their stated activity hangs together.
  • Structure complexity — the entities involved: companies, trusts, layered or cross-linked arrangements, and whether the complexity has a plausible commercial rationale.
  • Cash intensity — how much of the client's activity runs in physical cash, and how it is handled and banked.
  • Geography — overseas links: funds moving across borders, foreign counterparties, and higher-risk jurisdictions.
  • Delivery channel — how the firm engages the client: face to face with identity sighted, or remotely through intermediaries.
  • Source of funds — where the money for the engagement comes from, and how well its origin is evidenced.
  • PEP / sanctions indicators — connections to public office or sanctioned parties as surfaced in conversation. This is never a screening result — see section 7.

Each factor is interpreted through the firm's sector. What "structure complexity" means for a conveyancing file differs from a financial-advice fact-find, so each sector carries its own reading of the factors, its own expected onboarding topics, and its own red-flag indicators — grounded in AUSTRAC's published sector guidance.

3. The evidence rule

A factor may be rated Low, Medium, High, or Not covered — and a rating other than "Not covered" must carry evidence: a short verbatim quote from the meeting transcript with its timestamp. Paraphrase is not permitted inside evidence quotes. If the conversation did not address a factor, the factor is marked "Not covered" with a suggested follow-up question — it is never guessed.

The analysis is explicitly instructed to be conservative: a rating of Medium or High must be genuinely supported by what was said, and the absence of a risk indicator is treated as normal, not suspicious.

4. Open items — what the conversation didn't cover

Each sector defines the topics an onboarding conversation is expected to cover. Topics the meeting did not reach become open items on the assessment — visible on the report until a practitioner resolves them (by phone, email, or a follow-up meeting, each recorded with a note). Thinness is surfaced, never papered over: a short or incomplete conversation produces an assessment that says so.

5. How the overall rating is derived

The overall rating is the worst of the factor ratings — a deliberate, simple, defensible rule:

  • any factor rated High → overall High;
  • otherwise any Medium → overall Medium;
  • otherwise Low.

"Not covered" factors never lift the overall rating — they surface as open items instead. There is no hidden weighting or score blending: one genuinely high-risk dimension makes the file high-risk until a practitioner, with reasons, determines otherwise.

6. The practitioner's role — and the audit trail

The draft is the start, not the verdict. In the dashboard the practitioner can override any factor rating (a reason is required), resolve open items, and then sign. Every override, resolution and signature is attributed and timestamped, and the report is versioned — edit a signed report and it must be signed again. The signed review, not the AI draft, is the firm's assessment, and the full trail of who changed what is retained with the record.

7. What the assessment is not

Liisten does not perform identity verification, PEP screening, or sanctions screening. The "PEP / sanctions indicators" factor reflects only what arose in conversation — for example, a client mentioning a relative in public office — and always directs confirmation to the firm's screening provider. Liisten's reports state this on their face. Nor is any report legal advice; the methodology maps to AUSTRAC guidance, but obligations under a firm's own AML/CTF program remain the firm's.

8. The AI, plainly

The analysis is performed by Claude (Anthropic), invoked through AWS Bedrock in Sydney — the recording, transcript, and analysis never leave Australia. The model works from the meeting transcript and this methodology's rules; it does not browse, does not learn from your data, and each firm's material is processed independently. Three design choices constrain the known failure modes of language models: the evidence rule (no rating without a verbatim, timestamped quote), the "Not covered" rule (no guessing), and the human sign-off (no assessment becomes the firm's without review). Model and methodology identifiers are printed on every assessment.

9. Versioning

Every assessment permanently records the methodology version and model version that produced it. When this methodology materially changes, its version number increments and the change is noted below — so a rating produced under v1.0 can always be read against the v1.0 rules, whenever it is examined.
