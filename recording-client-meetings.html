Recording client meetings: why — and how to ask

Your AML/CTF obligations ask for evidence, not just conclusions — and the evidence is the conversation you were already having. Here's why practitioners record the onboarding meeting, what the law says, and the exact words that make the ask a non-event.

Why record the meeting at all?

A risk rating with nothing behind it is a conclusion, not an assessment. When a rating is questioned — by an auditor, by AUSTRAC, by your own compliance officer three years later — the question is always the same: what was this based on?

AUSTRAC's own customer due diligence forms tell practitioners to answer the risk questions from "your interactions" with the client. The onboarding meeting is that interaction. Recording it, with consent, turns the interaction itself into the evidence: what your client actually said, in their own words, timestamped — behind every rating you sign. See how ratings are determined →

A record made in the meeting also beats one reconstructed afterwards. A form filled in an hour later is a memory test with a compliance record as the output.

Is it legal?

Yes — recording a conversation where everyone consents is lawful across Australia. Liisten is consent-first by design: recording doesn't start until consent is captured on the record, so the proof of consent lives with the meeting it belongs to, permanently. Recording laws vary by state; when in doubt, ask before recording — which is exactly what the flow makes you do.

How to ask — without making it a big deal

The ask works when it's one sentence, before you start, said the way you'd say anything routine. The law makes the ask for you — you never have to own it personally. Use whichever fits your voice:

The compliance frame

"New AML laws mean I need to keep a proper record of this conversation — I'll record it rather than take notes the whole way through, so I can give you my full attention. That okay?"

The service frame

"I record these meetings so nothing you tell me gets lost or misremembered — you'll get a proper record of what we agreed."

The casual frame

"I'll pop my phone on the table to record us — beats me scribbling notes the whole time. All good?"

Three habits that keep it a non-event

  • Ask before you start, not mid-meeting. Consent is the first thing on the recording, then it's never mentioned again.
  • Don't apologise for it. An apologetic ask invites refusal. Said plainly, it's as routine as "do you take milk?"
  • Put the phone down and leave it. The phone flat on the desk, untouched, disappears within a minute — faster than a keyboard ever does.

If your client says no

Don't record — it's their call, and Liisten never records without consent. You can still document the meeting your usual way, or upload audio captured elsewhere through the dashboard. One refusal doesn't break anything.

And exercise your normal professional judgement: how a client responds to routine record-keeping during a conversation about their identity, their business and their source of funds is information like any other. Note it in the file.

What your client gets out of it

  • Your full attention. No keyboard between you, no scribbling — a practitioner who's listening.
  • An accurate record. What was actually said and agreed — not what someone remembered an hour later.
  • A reviewed record. Nothing becomes the firm's record until a practitioner has read it, corrected anything, and signed. Recordings are processed onshore in Sydney and never used to train AI. Security details →

For accountants & bookkeepers

Your onboarding conversation already covers what customer due diligence asks — the business, the structure, the cash, where funds come from. Recording it means the answers on your CDD form have the client's own words behind them, instead of a tick with nothing underneath. Liisten for accountants →

For financial advisers

The fact-find is the CDD interview in all but name. Record it and the wealth, structures and source-of-funds discussion you already run becomes the documented evidence — without adding a single question to the meeting. Liisten for advisers →

For lawyers & conveyancers

A recorded intake is a confidential communication with your client like any other — privilege attaches to it the way it attaches to your file note. Many firms record the intake and CDD portion of the matter and handle substantive advice however their practice prefers; the pause control makes that split simple. Liisten for legal practices →

For real estate agents

Your CDD moments are conversations — appraisals, buyer chats, offer negotiations — often away from a desk. The phone you already carry captures the conversation that answers the vendor and buyer questions AUSTRAC asks. Liisten for real estate →
